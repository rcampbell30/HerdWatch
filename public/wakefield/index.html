Andrew Wakefield: what the evidence shows

The 1998 MMR paper was retracted, Wakefield was removed from the UK medical register, and a BMJ investigation described the article as fraudulent. Accountability is strongest when every claim is sourced and broader consequences are not invented.

Why this page exists: the MMR-autism story remains part of public discussion about vaccine confidence. It is reasonable to document that history clearly.

This page does not estimate deaths caused by one person, claim that one episode explains every later fall in coverage, or convert a complex public-health history into an unsupported number. It separates the established record from inference.

Documented timeline

February 1998
The Lancet publishes a 12-child case series
The article described 12 children and proposed a new syndrome. A case series of this size and design could not establish that MMR caused autism. The journal record now identifies the article as retracted. View the Lancet record.
2004 onward
Investigative reporting examines conflicts and records
Brian Deer's reporting investigated undisclosed litigation-related work, financial interests and differences between the published account and clinical records. The precise evidence and chronology are set out in the later BMJ series rather than paraphrased here as an unsourced allegation.
January 2010
The General Medical Council announces findings of fact
After its longest-running fitness-to-practise hearing at the time, the GMC panel announced detailed findings of fact, including findings of dishonesty and irresponsibility against Wakefield. Read the contemporary BMJ report.
February 2010
The Lancet fully retracts the paper
The retraction followed the GMC findings. This happened before the separate decision to remove Wakefield from the medical register.
May 2010
Wakefield is struck off
The GMC found Wakefield guilty of serious professional misconduct and directed that his name be erased from the medical register. Retraction and removal from the register should not be collapsed into one February event. Read the BMJ sanction report.
January 2011
The BMJ investigation calls the article fraudulent
The investigation compared the publication with available clinical records and reported material discrepancies in timelines, diagnoses and recruitment. Read “How the case against the MMR vaccine was fixed”.

Financial interests, stated carefully

The BMJ investigation reported that Wakefield undertook legal-aid-funded work connected to planned litigation and that relevant interests were not disclosed in the Lancet paper. It also examined plans and intellectual-property interests relating to alternative products.

Rather than asserting that a later aggregate payment total was all received before publication, this site links to the underlying investigation. That lets readers distinguish when payments began, what the total represented and which facts were established by which source.

The present UK context

The Wakefield episode contributed to a major loss of confidence in MMR. It is not accurate, however, to say that national coverage fell to 73% or that it never recovered. Official reporting places England's MMR1 low point at 79.9% in 2003–04, followed by substantial recovery. Read the National Audit Office figures.

The UK later lost measles elimination status based on 2024 transmission data. UKHSA discusses current coverage, outbreaks and elimination status without attributing the entire modern situation to one historical actor. Access barriers, inequalities, service disruption and contemporary misinformation also matter.

Read the current UKHSA elimination assessment

Why restraint matters

The established facts are serious enough. Unsupported death totals, fabricated-looking quotations or single-cause stories make accurate criticism easier to dismiss.

The defensible conclusion: the original article did not establish an MMR-autism link; it was retracted; Wakefield was struck off; and the BMJ investigation described the article as fraudulent. Large subsequent studies do not find that MMR increases autism risk.
